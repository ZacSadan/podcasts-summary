wanted to take young, healthy UC Berkeley undergraduates there at the peak of their cognitive abilities. Right? 18 to 22 years old. Their brains are perfect textbook. They're perfect, and they're insanely smart. And I'm like, I want to make them smarter. We ran the seven week training program. And lo and behold, those 18 to 22 year olds that are already perfect became better, so to speak. […] Their attention spans got better, general fluid intelligence increased, complex working memory increased. And this happened, mind you, in seven weeks.” Dr Yousef's claim appears to refer to her 2018 PhD dissertation at UC Berkeley (Yousef, 2018), which has not been published in a peer-reviewed journal. In this study, healthy UC Berkeley students completed a seven-week attention-regulation course combining weekly classes, a training game and practice in daily life (n = 84 completers), and were compared with three control groups (n = 27–39 each). The trained group improved more than all control groups on a 4-minute test of sustained attention (SART) and on a complex working-memory task (Operation Span) and reported better every day executive function. However, it did not improve more than the controls on three simpler memory tasks, and the study included no test of fluid intelligence. The claim that the programme increased 'general fluid intelligence' is therefore not supported by the study. The attention and complex working-memory findings are consistent with her description, but several features of the study limit how far they can be relied on. First, the design was not fully randomised, and the groups were also unequal in size (107 accepted into the training group compared with 30–44 in each control group). Analyses included only participants who completed the study, and dropout was also uneven. Second, the measured gains may reflect what was taught and not a general improvement in cognitive capacity. Third, the reported effect sizes (d = 1.07 for attention and d = 0.58 for working memory) describe the trained group's change from before to after training, not the difference between the trained and control groups, and therefore overstate the effect attributable to the training. The study also did not report baseline scores or group means in the text, and measured outcomes only once, immediately after training, so it is unknown whether any gains persisted. Finally, the results have not been published in a peer-reviewed journal. These findings should be regarded as preliminary, and larger, randomised, independent studies would be needed to confirm that such training improves attention or working memory beyond the tasks used to measure it. Reference 1. How Long Workers Stay on One Screen “So he's sitting there, I swear, Steven, he probably got 30s, you know, the average, there was a study done, the new average. And this is, mind you, already years old. The stat 47 seconds is the average amount of time a knowledge worker stays focused on one screen before another one distracts him.” The 47-second figure comes from the work of Gloria Mark. Using computer-logging software that recorded when workers switched between windows and screens, her group found that the average time spent on a single screen before switching fell from about 2.5 minutes in 2004 to about 75 seconds in 2012, and to an average of around 47 seconds in studies conducted from 2016 onwards (Mark et al., 2016). This measure describes how long people stay on one screen or window, not how long they stay focused before being distracted. Many switches are deliberate and task-related (for example, moving between a document and a data source), and the studies involved relatively small samples of information workers in specific organisations. Mark's research does, however, also show that frequent switching is associated with higher stress and lower productivity. Reference 2. How Much Sleep Adults Need “So that's the thing when you don't get enough sleep and mind you, that's a minimum of seven hours a night, right? We always want to shoot for 7 to 9 hours of high quality sleep.” A joint consensus statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommends that adults sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis to promote optimal health, noting that sleeping less than 7 hours is associated with adverse outcomes including weight gain, diabetes, hypertension, depression and impaired performance (Watson et al., 2015). The US National Sleep Foundation recommends 7–9 hours for adults aged 18–64 and 7–8 hours for those aged 65 and over (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015). These recommendations are population- level guidance. Individual sleep needs vary, and a small number of people appear to function well on less sleep for genetic reasons. The guidance also emphasises regularity and sleep quality, not only duration. References 3, 4. Effects of Sleep Loss on Cognition, Mood and Appearance “If you're not getting enough sleep, it's impacting your cognitive performance, your emotional regulation. So you'll be irascible, you know, in the office and with other people. It's so the whole thing. You even look worse. Like the system is messed up. So sleep is for sure the foundation.” Controlled sleep-restriction experiments show that limiting sleep to 6 hours or fewer per night for two weeks produces cumulative deficits in sustained attention and working memory comparable to one or two nights of total sleep deprivation, while participants underestimate how impaired they are (Van Dongen et al., 2003). Sleep loss also affects emotional processing: after a night without sleep, amygdala responses to negative images are about 60% greater and functional connectivity with the prefrontal cortex is reduced (Yoo et al., 2007), and a meta-analysis of experimental studies found that sleep loss increases negative mood and reduces positive mood (Tomaso et al., 2021). The comment that people 'look worse' also has experimental support. In a study published in the BMJ, untrained observers rated photographs of people taken after sleep deprivation as less healthy, more tired and less attractive than photographs taken after normal sleep (Axelsson et al., 2010), and a later study found that people were less willing to socialise with those who had been sleep restricted (Sundelin et al., 2017). References 5-9. Monotasking, Distraction and Memory “Focus and attention are so fundamental to all things personal and professional. Mind you, that means any kind of distraction that is diminishing your focus is impacting your memory.” Human cognition exhibits severe constraints when attempting to execute multiple demanding tasks simultaneously. In the 'psychological refractory period' paradigm, responses to a second task are delayed when it follows a first task closely, suggesting a central processing bottleneck (Pashler, 1994). People can combine well-practised, automatic activities (such as walking while talking), so 'monotasking' is best understood as applying to tasks that require deliberate attention. Distraction during the acquisition phase markedly reduces subsequent episodic retention across diverse retrieval formats. Unlike retrieval, which remains comparatively resilient to secondary distractions, encoding requires general cognitive resources, leaving it vulnerable to disruption. References 10-19. Changes in Attention Span Since the Smartphone “And attention spans. Our ability to sustain attention has been linearly decreasing since the advent of this thing. And I don't want people to think it's just phones necessarily. It's this. It's notifications in general.” The scientific literature identifies clear transient attentional disruptions from device interactions, but robust longitudinal evidence confirming a permanent, population-level decline in sustained attention capacity remains absent. Studies do show mobile alerts and physical presence induce transient cognitive disruption during active task execution. Research also links heavy smartphone and media multitasking to poorer performance on some attention tasks, and a recent meta-analysis found that heavier short-form video use is associated with poorer attention (Nguyen et al., 2025). However, most of this evidence is cross-sectional and cannot show that smartphones caused a population-wide decline (Wilmer et al., 2017). A 'linear' decrease since the advent of the smartphone has not been demonstrated directly in the literature. References 20-24. Women and the Tendency to Say Yes “So I'm a default yes person. Um, I also find for women, it can be our default is naturally, I think more to yes, especially when it comes to people pleasing and other people.” Evidence suggests women are more often asked, and more likely to agree, to take on tasks that benefit others but carry little career reward. In a series of laboratory and field studies, Babcock and colleagues (2017) found that women were more likely than men to volunteer for 'non- promotable' tasks, and that both men and women were more likely to ask women to do them. Importantly, when groups were all-female or all-male, the gender difference disappeared, which the authors interpret as reflecting shared expectations about who will say yes. Personality research consistently finds that women score modestly higher than men on average on agreeableness, a trait associated with cooperation and a desire to maintain social harmony, but the distributions of men and women overlap substantially (Weisberg et al., 2011). The evidence therefore supports a tendency, on average, for women to agree to more requests, but it does not show that this is 'natural' as opposed to shaped by social roles and expectations (Eagly and Wood, 1999). References 25-27. Focus, Human Survival and Threat Detection “What do you think has led to human survival? It is, in fact, our ability to focus […] We got to fire and weapons and things like this through intense focus. So the human brain has evolved to literally focus and tune out noise…” “But also, while I'm doing all this focusing, I got to keep an ear and an eye out for whether or not I might die and be attacked. And I need to see threats coming from a long mile away. So we have a hyper vigilant, both auditory and visual system.” The idea that sustained, focused attention was central to human evolutionary success is a plausible narrative but is difficult to test directly. Anthropologists typically emphasise a combination of factors, including cooperation, social learning, language, tool use and the transmission of culture (Henrich, 2016). The control of fire, for instance, appears to have emerged gradually and to have been sustained through cultural transmission over many generations. The second part of the claim, that human attentional systems are tuned to detect potential threats, is better supported. Experimental studies show that people detect threat-relevant stimuli such as snakes, spiders and angry faces faster than neutral stimuli (Öhman et al., 2001), and attention research distinguishes between goal-directed ('top-down') attention and stimulus-driven ('bottom-up') attention that is captured automatically by sudden or salient events (Corbetta and Shulman, 2002). This stimulus-driven system helps explain why movement, sounds and notifications in the environment are hard to ignore. References 28-30. How Much of the Cortex Is Devoted to Vision and Hearing “Do you know about a third of the human brain like I and I say, the cortical real estate. So real estate across this human cortex is dedicated just to looking and listening. That's a lot.” “And that visual stimulation, as we were talking about before, huge part of your cortex is your visual cortex, your occipital lobe.” Vision occupies a large share of the human cerebral cortex. Mapping studies indicate that visual areas cover roughly a quarter to a third of the human cortex, a smaller proportion than in macaque monkeys, where visual areas occupy about half (Van Essen, 2004; Wandell et al., 2007). Auditory cortex occupies a considerably smaller area. The occipital lobe does contain the primary visual cortex, but visual processing extends well beyond it into the temporal and parietal lobes. The implication that the size of the visual cortex makes us especially susceptible to colourful screens is an inference (see the section on screen colour saturation). References 31, 32. Using Phones to Avoid Uncomfortable Emotions “Now there's two ways to solve what you're overwhelmed about, right? You could either deal with it head on, which sounds like a heck of a lot of work, or you could avoid it. No emotional avoidance. Now, what's the fastest and easiest way in the modern world? We can avoid what's going on inside of us?” “It is natural to numb. It is natural to avoid feelings of discomfort. […] They notice that every single time they sit down and there's a moment of silence, they grab for it. This is it's a natural thing because what our phones have done for us is created an exit, a very fast, cheap exit to reality.” Experiential avoidance, the unwillingness to remain in contact with painful internal experiences, serves as a primary vulnerability factor for problematic device habits. In clinical and community cohorts, individuals with elevated experiential avoidance often rely on accessible digital media as a maladaptive safety behaviour. In a sample of 294 adults, excessive smartphone use acted as a maladaptive safety behaviour that significantly exacerbated the association between experiential avoidance and anxiety (Gordon et al., 2021). A 6-month longitudinal study revealed that experiential avoidance directly predicted transitions into high-risk addiction subgroups among adolescents (Song et al., 2026). Interestingly, a prospective multi-wave evaluation found that initial smartphone dependency predicted later increases in experiential avoidance, suggesting a mutually reinforcing cycle (Gerdan and Akgül, 2025). Emerging studies confirm that negative emotional states, specifically acute tension, social isolation, and an absence of environmental stimulation, prompt immediate mobile device engagement (Orsolini et al., 2025). Most of this evidence is correlational, so the direction of the relationship is uncertain: people who are anxious or low in mood may use their phones more, but heavy use may also maintain or worsen these states. References 33-43. Smartphone Use, Addiction and Withdrawal “And I teach a class, by the way, on digital dependence. And we do digital detoxes in the class. It's a behavior change course. So you have like hundreds and hundreds of young people going through withdrawal at the same time […] There should be no shame in the fact that a lot of people are finding themselves in this moment in time, very much addicted and dependent to either scrolling or their phones in general.” Researchers remain divided on whether problematic smartphone use should be classified as an addiction; critics argue that it lacks clear evidence of tolerance, withdrawal and severe functional impairment and that labelling everyday overuse as addiction risks over-pathologising normal behaviour (Panova and Carbonell, 2018). On withdrawal, experimental studies of brief smartphone abstinence have reported increased craving and, in some cases, anxiety, but the effects are generally modest and do not resemble withdrawal from. Radtke et al. (2022) conducted a systematic literature review of digital detox interventions and concluded that their effects on health, subjective well-being, and social relationships vary considerably across studies, with evidence showing positive outcomes, null results, and negative consequences. The review systematically mapped findings across distinct domains of subjective and psychological well-being: • Depression: In three extracted studies, intentional breaks from social media or general smartphone use yielded a significant decline in depression symptoms; • Anxiety: Out of four studies evaluating anxiety via standardised inventories, three observed no detectable effect, while one study reported an anxiety decrease restricted solely to participants with mild-to-moderate baseline symptoms; • Mood and boredom: A 24-hour digital detox intervention had no effect on mood, whereas an enforced seven-day social media abstinence trial provoked higher boredom levels; • Mental well-being: Results were contradictory; one study reported a medium-sized increase in mental well-being after a 7-day detox, another reported gains in flourishing among depressed cohorts, while an experimental trial found no psychological well-being benefit; • Craving and withdrawal: All three studies examining withdrawal dynamics demonstrated that craving significantly increased during and immediately following abstinence from smartphones or social media. Critical methodological shortcomings across the existing evidence base include: • Most interventions relied on subjective participant recall due to a lack of device-based measurements to verify compliance; • Studies demonstrated an absence of short- or long-term follow-ups, leaving post- intervention sustainability unknown; • Complete abstinence requirements created unrealistic demands that ignore modern functional reliance on mobile connectivity. The Electric Shock Study on Being Alone With Our Thoughts Steven Bartlett: “So essentially the study is people get electrocuted before the study starts, and then they're put in a room on their own with a button that would electrocute them again. […] many people continue to shock themselves over and over again. And in the study, men shock themselves more than women. One particular guy in the study electrocuted himself hundreds of times […] And the conclusion from the study is that we'd rather electrocute ourselves than sit alone in silence these days.” The study described is Wilson et al. (2014), which tested whether people left alone with their thoughts for 15 minutes would choose an unpleasant stimulus, and they found that some did. The key result was not that most people chose shocks, but that a substantial minority did: among 42 participants who had said they would pay to avoid being shocked again, 18 people self-administered at least one shock, while 57% never shocked themselves at all. Wilson et al. interpreted this as evidence that many people preferred some stimulation, even negative stimulation, to doing nothing but thinking. A later commentary argued that this interpretation was too strong, because the same data also show that a majority did not shock themselves and spent nearly all the 15-minute period simply thinking. The clearest subgroup difference was by sex: 67% of men (12 of 18) gave themselves at least one shock, versus 25% of women (6 of 24). Among men, the reported range was 0 to 4 shocks and the mean was 1.47, excluding one extreme outlier; among women, the range was 0 to 9 shocks and the mean was 1.00. Wilson et al. suggested the sex difference probably reflected higher sensation- seeking in men, not necessarily a sex-specific dislike of thinking itself. The extreme case was one man who administered 190 shocks to himself during the 15- minute thinking period. Wilson et al. treated him as an outlier and excluded him from the reported male mean and standard deviation. That participant is real and comes from the original report, but he was highly atypical. The commentary emphasised that only a small minority gave themselves more than two shocks, so the 190-shock case should not be mistaken for the typical behaviour in the study. References 60, 61. Effects of Social Isolation on the Brain “We are pack animals. At the end of the day, you and I can't survive in a silo on our own. We need each other. And the human brain actually, by the way, atrophies if you're left in isolation for too long. If you do not speak to other human beings, you do not interact with other human beings. When humans are left in isolation, really bad things start to happen to the brain.” Neuroimaging evidence links chronic social isolation to lower total brain volume as well as localised cortical thinning and subcortical contractions. In a population-based panel study of 1,992 older adults followed over ~6 years, both baseline isolation and increases in isolation over time predicted smaller hippocampal volumes and regional cortical thinning, which the authors explicitly characterised as human brain atrophy. Whole-brain analyses show specific clusters of thinning across middle frontal and praecuneus regions as isolation scores increase. Similarly, investigations using the UK Biobank found that individuals experiencing prolonged objective isolation exhibited lower GMV in temporal, frontal, and hippocampal structures, independently of subjective loneliness or depressive symptoms. In an analysis of 8,896 dementia- free older adults, individuals with infrequent social contact displayed smaller volumes in the hippocampus, temporal lobe, occipital lobe, and amygdala, alongside elevated white matter lesion burden. Regional volumetric reductions in the hippocampus have also been documented following extended periods of confined polar isolation. The term atrophy accurately captures the macrostructural metric observed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), namely, progressive volumetric reduction and cortical thinning relative to cranial volume. However, describing prolonged social isolation as the definitive causal driver requires caution due to methodological limitations in human research: • Reverse Causality: Preclinical dementia and early neurodegenerative pathology often reduce motivation and social engagement before noticeable cognitive decline manifests, introducing potential reverse causation; • Subjective vs Objective Dissociation: Perceived social isolation (loneliness) primarily modulates functional connectivity and default mode network integrity, whereas objective isolation correlates more consistently with diffuse grey matter loss; • Confounding Risk Factors: Cardiovascular morbidity, systemic inflammation, chronic stress, and depressive symptoms frequently co-occur with isolation and account for substantial variance in brain volume loss; • Cellular Mechanisms: Animal models confirm that isolation induces oligodendrocyte remodelling, impaired neurogenesis, and reduced brain-derived neurotrophic factor, but these cellular processes cannot be verified non-invasively in human MRI studies. References 62-76. Connection, Novelty and Learning as Human Needs “We all do have a natural, fundamental need for learning and novelty because again, survival, we have this natural, fundamental need to want to learn things and to grow, because this new piece of information might be a huge unlock for me to thrive even more in this world.” Across neuroscience, psychology, and evolutionary accounts, humans and other animals often seek novelty and information without external reward, and information itself can carry intrinsic value. Several reviews explicitly frame curiosity as evolutionarily useful because it helps organisms reduce uncertainty, discover rewards, avoid dangers, and build adaptive behavioural repertoires that improve survival. References 77-82. Screen Colour Saturation and Attention “So there are actual technology looks more visually stimulating. […] So it's super, super stimulated by all of that color saturation. So it's like, oh my gosh, they think of like a casino.” “First you make the phone in and of it in and of itself less neurologically stimulating, literally the color saturation of your phone, the light that's emitted from the screen has been designed to keep your eyes locked on the device.” Device Use Across the studies, the clearest pattern is that removing colour from smartphones reduces use time, while more visually rich, saturated displays appear to sustain engagement. In parallel, laboratory and interface studies show that saturation and colour salience reliably shape where users look and how quickly they detect or process on-screen content, but brightness alone does not produce a simple, uniform increase in attention. The mechanism proposed across these studies is not that people stop checking their phones, but that phones become less gratifying and easier to put down once saturated colour is removed. The app-level effects are narrower than the overall screen-time effect. Social media and internet browsing declined in one grayscale study, while video time did not (Holte and Ferraro, 2020). A later study found no significant moderation by image-based versus text/audio app category, although the mean reduction was larger for image-based apps, which fits the broader idea that colour-rich content is more affected (Dekker and Baumgartner, 2023). Grayscale also improved perceived control, reduced perceived overuse, online vigilance, and stress, but did not improve productivity or sleep quality in that sample. Attention Capture Across classic colour-attention experiments, high saturation and brightness often attract the most immediate attention. In one foundational study, maximum saturation and maximum brightness drew the most choices across backgrounds, and brightness-saturation levels mattered more than hue alone. More recent reading and interface work reaches a similar conclusion for early orienting: warm, highly saturated colours speed pre-attentive capture, and colour and background redistribute fixation patterns across app layouts. Brightness and Contrast Evidence on brightness is more mixed because many studies find that contrast, luminance balance, or context matter more than absolute brightness. In time-perception experiments, higher contrast lengthened perceived duration, whereas increasing target brightness actually shortened it, leading the authors to conclude that contrast was the primary determinant. Peripheral alert studies similarly found that high visual intensity was not necessary for strongest visibility, and that medium contrast could maintain attention without the irritation or cognitive load of stronger signals. Brightness can still affect attention and fatigue under some conditions. Higher melanopic luminance made displays appear brighter and improved alertness and attention markers, but it also increased visual fatigue. Other display studies found that low brightness at night reduced visual fatigue, medium brightness in AR gave the best performance with least fatigue, and screen luminance interacted with room illumination. An fNIRS study adds that high brightness and saturation were rated as more attractive, while lower saturation increased pleasantness, suggesting that attention-grabbing settings are not always the most comfortable or preferred. Overall, the evidence supports a fairly strong claim for colour saturation: it increases visual salience, captures attention, and appears to prolong time spent on devices. For brightness, the evidence is more conditional: it can alter alertness and attention, but its effects are often entangled with contrast, ambient light, task demands, and visual fatigue. Greyscale Mode and Reduced Screen Time “And research from my lab shows, by the way, this is for working professionals like you just going into grayscale, they spent one hour less on their phone.” Steven Bartlett: “Have they ever tested that engagement with devices when they are in grayscale or in a different color saturation?” “I've tested it. So in my lab, we took a large sample of healthy working professionals, and all I did was turn their phones into gray and asked them to stay in gray for three weeks. That's it. And we saw, on average, people spent one hour less on their phones every single day. That's seven hours a week.” We could not find a published, peer-reviewed report of the greyscale study from Dr Yousef's lab, so the claimed one-hour reduction cannot be independently verified. Published studies support the direction of the effect but report smaller reductions. In a study of about 200 US college students, switching to greyscale reduced screen time by an average of about 39 minutes a day (Holte and Ferraro, 2020). In a pre-registered Dutch study, a one-week greyscale intervention reduced smartphone use by about 20 minutes a day, with larger reductions for image-based apps, but the number of times participants unlocked their phones did not change (Dekker and Baumgartner, 2023). A randomised field experiment comparing several screen-time strategies also found that design-friction interventions such as greyscale led to an immediate, significant reduction in objectively measured screen time relative to a control condition (Zimmermann and Sobolev, 2023). Thus, greyscale appears to be a simple, effective tool, with evidence suggesting typical reductions of roughly 20–40 minutes per day, and long-term effects have not been well studied. References 101-104. Variable Rewards and Addictive Behaviour “And by the way, there's an entire field, intermittent variable rewards. You get these jackpots once in a while, right.” “Human beings, believe it or not. Do not love consistent reward. We become more addicted when it's in fact intermittent and somewhat variable.” “If it showed you every single thing that you wanted to see. […] Believe it or not, you'd be less likely to get addicted.” The underlying principle is well established in behavioural psychology. Intermittent, variable rewards do not mean people dislike all consistent rewards, it means unpredictability can amplify wanting beyond what equally sized predictable rewards produce. The clearest evidence base is gambling, where variable-ratio or random-ratio schedules are treated as a central motivational feature of slot-machine play and other games of chance. Direct human experiments are limited, but several papers extend the same logic to social media, gaming, and internet use. Social platforms deliver unpredictable likes, notifications, and messages that are argued to maintain habitual checking through variable reinforcement, and repeated social rewards appear to heighten sensitivity to reward cues. Gaming and loot boxes are described as parallel cases where uncertain reward delivery resembles gambling. At the same time, addictive behaviour also depends on immediacy, delay discounting, cue salience, gratification, and limited alternatives, not reward variability alone. References 105-112. Love Bombing and Intermittent Reinforcement “I have a whole theory on like people who are in toxic relationships, like seriously think about like love bombing. This is the science of love bombing. Like, I'm an asshole. I'm an asshole. I'm like, ghosting you and then bam! Oh, I hit you with a bunch of love. And people are like, oh, my. And then they become addicted to these toxic fucking relationships. Similar concepts.” The literature supports a clear association between trauma bonding and cycles of alternating affection and harm, although much of the evidence is observational, qualitative, or theoretical. Love bombing appears to strengthen emotional attachment when it is part of a broader pattern of coercion, inconsistent affection, and power imbalance. A useful way to connect the two concepts is as a sequence: idealisation first, then unpredictability, then dependency. Several papers describe love bombing as an early stage marked by intense attention and fast-tracked emotional closeness, followed by devaluation, withdrawal, control, or discard. Trauma-bonding papers describe the next step: once affection becomes intermittent, the alternation of reward and harm strengthens fixation and makes leaving harder. References 113-118. Time on Social Media and Loneliness “What is social media has done is it's created the illusion of social connection because we have the data to support. The longer you spend on these apps and on these platforms, the lonelier you are.” There is evidence of an association between more time on social media and more loneliness, but for time-based measures the average effect is small and heterogeneous. Evidence is stronger for problematic social media use than for simple time spent: a meta-analysis of longitudinal studies found small bidirectional effects between loneliness and problematic media use, and a meta-analysis in adolescents and young adults found a moderate cross-sectional correlation for problematic use versus a much smaller one for time spent. References 119-125. How Much We Retain From Short-Form Content “This is the hijack of social media. And short form content is you think you're getting that. But I promise you, just like with the Skittles, you end up getting off and you don't remember anything that you, by the way, you just watched test it. […] And this is you think you're learning. It's an illusion.” In the literature, the strongest pattern is that fragmented, rapidly switching short-form videos in social-media-like formats tend to reduce memory accuracy, increase forgetting, and impair prospective memory relative to continuous long-form viewing, while well-structured educational microvideos often improve learning compared with long unsegmented lectures. A second pattern is that videos alone can create an illusion of understanding: learners often report similar or higher confidence, focus, or perceived retention than long-form conditions even when objective performance is worse or follow-up tasks are needed to calibrate self-assessment. The direct retention evidence does support the claim for one important subtype: short videos that are fragmented, rapidly switched, or feed-like. In matched-content lab studies, these formats produced poorer immediate memory, higher forgetting, weaker event segmentation, and worse intention recall than long videos. The proposed mechanism is consistent across several studies: short-form feeds heighten bottom-up attentional capture while weakening top-down control, semantic integration, and network coupling needed for durable encoding. That pattern does not generalise to all short videos. In education studies, brief videos of roughly 5-10 minutes, or longer videos split into meaningful segments, often improved learning gains, exam scores, engagement, or delayed retention compared with long unsegmented lectures. Broader higher-education evidence also suggests video usually helps learning when added to teaching, although that literature is about video overall, not specifically ultra-short feed content. References 126-136. Short-Form Content and Attention Span “Next is choosing long form over short form.” “No, but honestly, it's true. It's really it's not like made up short form content has even been shown, by the way, in some cases to increase attention span.” The evidence suggests that heavier or more problematic short-form video use is associated with worse sustained attention, greater distractibility, and weaker executive control, especially in students and young people. Writing for a business and innovation audience, May argues that in an age of excess, effective solutions often come from removing what is confusing, wasteful or superfluous. Alternating between cognitive tasks reliably degrades both speed and accuracy compared to continuous single-task execution. In reaction-time paradigms, local switch costs typically introduce an immediate latency penalty ranging from 50 to several hundred milliseconds per individual switch. Providing explicit advance preparation time reduces these temporal delays, but an unavoidable residual switch cost consistently persists even after several seconds of preparation. In addition to local transitions, operating within mixed-task environments incurs global "mixing costs" reflecting the cognitive overhead of maintaining competing task configurations in working memory. Concurrent multitasking between attention-demanding tasks is considered a myth because central executive processes operate largely as a serial bottleneck. Neuroimaging and behavioral findings show that sequential task switching and concurrent dual-tasking recruit distinct but overlapping frontoparietal networks, indicating that cognitive resources cannot simultaneously serve two un-automatized goals. • Central response selection limitations prevent simultaneous decision-making, forcing the cognitive system to alternate execution serially; • Task switching forces active task-set reconfiguration, requiring time to suppress obsolete rules and activate new parameters; • Lingering task-set inertia from prior actions produces proactive interference that impairs ongoing accuracy and working memory maintenance. Generalisation to Real-World Productivity Simple writing demonstrations, such as alternating between writing letters and numbers, illustrate the fundamental mechanics of cognitive interference, but they fail to capture the multi- dimensional structure of natural work settings. Controlled demonstrations capture transient, fine- grained task alternation, whereas workplace interruptions involve complex, dissimilar tasks spanning prolonged temporal blocks. Translating laboratory switch costs into macroeconomic productivity loss remains challenging because field studies vary widely in how they define and evaluate workplace disruption, presenteeism, and efficiency. Nevertheless, research examining everyday tasks, such as combining vehicle operation with typing or argumentative reasoning, confirms that multi-task interference substantially degrades real-world operational safety and performance. The second topic has the stronger direct evidence base, especially from lab experiments on attention and information sampling, while the first is more conceptual and often studied through serendipity, entrepreneurship, and luck beliefs. Opportunity Recognition Researchers describe serendipity as noticing something unexpected and then doing something useful with it, and not pure random luck. In this literature, the key personal qualities are alertness, curiosity, and intuition, because these help people notice weak cues that others might miss. Opportunity beliefs are treated as their own psychological category, separate from luck, good fortune, and bad fortune. One entrepreneurship paper argues that happier, more confident people tend to experience more serendipity, especially when their surroundings support social connection and independent thinking. A related conceptual paper proposes that superstitious or luck-based beliefs can reduce anxiety, make coincidences feel meaningful, and increase action, but can also become fatalistic and discourage action. Information Sampling Lab experiments show that after people make an initial choice, they tend to give more weight to later evidence that supports that choice. This effect appears in both perceptual tasks and numerical judgments, which suggests it is not limited to political or emotional topics. Researchers interpret this as a form of selective attention during later evidence evaluation. Other experiments show that bias appears not just in how evidence is weighted, but in what people choose to inspect. Participants sampled more information from the option they had already chosen, and this tendency became stronger when they felt more confident in their first decision. When the experimenter controlled what participants saw, this effect largely disappeared, which suggests that personal control over sampling is a key part of the bias. Broader Patterns Several papers argue that selective exposure does not stop once people encounter opposing information, because biased processing can continue during interpretation and later judgment. . Neural evidence suggests that conflicting evidence can still be encoded accurately in the brain, but it has less influence on later reports when it clashes with a previous choice. Some authors argue that these biases reflect both motivation and mental shortcuts and not simple irrationality. One model shows that if people simplify belief updating because tracking all dependencies is too memory-intensive, confirmation bias can emerge naturally during evaluation, assimilation, polarisation, and source selection. Final Notes Taken together, the literature describes a broad pattern in which opportunity noticing depends on cue detection, mindset, and context, while attention and evidence use are strongly shaped by prior choices, confidence, goals, and existing beliefs. The easiest-to-see findings come from experiments on sampling and selective exposure, while the luck and serendipity research adds a more interpretive picture of how people recognise and act on opportunities.
