RSA encryption significantly weakened We’ve seen for years that modern core cryptography algorithms are not broken, but they can be weakened. And that just happened when RSA cryptography is used for signing. In a paper dated September 20th, a team of researchers successfully attacked classical RSA cryptography using a technique to bypass the expected need to find a key’s prime factors. As we’ve often discussed, the surprising and enduring strength of protection offered by RSA-style cryptography relies upon the time-proven difficulty of factoring the massively long product of two prime numbers. That still stands because the researchers discovered a way around the factoring problem and the key forgery attack the researchers devised is practical 1024-bit RSA. Even for 2048- and 4096-bit keys, the method reduces the security of RSA to worrisome levels. The NSA, NIST and the EU’s Agency for Network and Information Security require that any cryptosystem should provide a level of no less than the equivalent of 128 bits of strength. The bad news here is that the application of this forgery attack reduces these levels to 65 bits, 90 bits and 119 bits for 1024-, 2048-, and 4096-bit keys respectively. Since further optimizations might be possible if AI or GPUs were employed. Fortunately, the signatures used to protect, for example, certificates remain completely safe and unaffected. The attack works only against what’s known as blind-signature implementations of RSA. Nearly all RSA in use today employs PKCS or PSS padding which adds data to the plaintext before it’s encrypted. That prevents ciphertext from being deterministic and renders it less vulnerable to side channel and similar attacks. However, some real-world systems do use blind- signature, also known as textbook, RSA. The best-known example is Privacy Pass, a protocol that allows users to authenticate themselves without revealing their identity. Privacy Pass is used by Apple, Cloudflare and others. Though the researchers’ attack took 1380 CPU core-years over five calendar months, that time might be sped up with higher-speed implementation. The now known to be vulnerable blind signature systems such as implementations of the Privacy Pass protocol should be updated to thwart these attacks. Since many billions of queries are required for the attack, rate-limiting might also succeed. Processor spectre mitigations are still not sufficient The researchers at the Systems and Network Security Group at VU Amsterdam, also known as VUSec, have discovered another new Spectre-style attack which is effective against all Security Now! #1099 10 Specter-mitigated processors. They call it “Branch Target Reuse” a practical spectre-v2 attack affecting the Just-In-Time (JIT) Engines via Stale Branch Prediction Entries. As an aside, recall that Microsoft was seeing so many problems arising inside the Edge/Chromium browser that they deliberately disabled Edge’s JIT compiler. It appears they called that one correctly. VUSec’s report writes: We present Branch Target Reuse (BTR), a new Spectre-v2 attack targeting just-in-time (JIT) compilers. BTR affects the JIT engines found in web browsers, language runtimes, and the operating system kernel, across multiple CPU vendors. We analyzed the attack surface of Linux cBPF, Oracle GraalVM and SpiderMonkey (the JIT engine of the Firefox browser), and built two end-to-end exploits against the Linux kernel. The key insight behind the attack is that, while modern CPUs restore architectural code coherence after self-modification, they do not necessarily invalidate stale indirect branch prediction entries (i.e., branch targets). In JIT engines, these stale targets can outlive the original code and later be reused when the code cache is repopulated, yielding a speculative execute-after-free primitive. This allows attackers to hijack speculative control flow to newly generated code at obsolete offsets, bypassing software hardening or reaching misaligned gadgets. Their FAQ about this attack asks: Is my system affected? Most likely. Indirect branch prediction is inherent to modern CPUs, and BTR exploits the desynchronization between the branch predictor and the actual state of the code. No current CPU has a mechanism to keep the two in sync, so until vendors add one, your CPU is vulnerable. We confirmed this behavior on every CPU we tested, covering Intel, AMD and Arm. Mitigation is left to software. How do I protect my system? Update your OS and software as soon as vendor patches are available. Both the Linux kernel and Oracle have released patches. As we have seen over and over and over since the first appearance of the Spectre and Meltdown attacks, processor designers who were attempting to squeeze every last possible ounce of performance from their chips noticed that code tended to reuse its execution paths and branch targets. So they added predictors and caches into the core of their designs. These features really did improve code performance by making their chips appear to already know which branch the code would take or where it would be branching to. The problem was that these slight changes in behavior could later be detected by adversarial code running on the same processor. Such code could abuse the hints left behind to breach privacy, containment and security. We’ve never wanted to sacrifice performance but the practical power of these attacks have been demonstrated many times so that processor designers have needed to work with software systems to create hybrid — and thus no longer entirely transparent — solutions. Teaching machines to love Because machine intelligence comes from a fundamentally different process than human intelligence, we cannot assume it adheres to human principles by default, or generalizes from them in a human-like manner. The core problem in AI research is that of alignment - getting the AI to “try to do the right thing” by human standards. For the purpose of organizing practical research directions, I find it useful to distinguish goal alignment and value alignment. Goal alignment is broadly: “does the AI try to accomplish the goal set before it?”. This can include things like adherence to an instruction hierarchy , or the ability to communicate and collaborate with people, to attempt to understand their objectives. This set of directions has been extremely practically relevant. Value alignment is a more intrinsic property of the model. It is the ability to hold and generalize from a high-level set of principles; to act “reasonably” even when given unclear or conflicting objectives, or placed in unfamiliar or adversarial situations. An aligned AI should act with honesty and integrity, and love for humanity. Of course, the boundary between value and goal alignment can be blurry, and truly caring Security Now! #1099 16 about goals requires attempting to infer the intent and values underlying them. However, generally when I talk about the long-term importance of alignment research, I am referring to value alignment. The fundamental challenge of AI alignment is generalization. As machines become smarter, they find themselves working on higher-level concepts, and placed in environments increasingly different from those they encountered in training. They can fail at generalizing from the values taught and reinforced in their training process to those new situations; and it can be hard for us to be sure how they will act. Crucially, we need future AIs to continue to hold human values regardless of whether they believe they’re under human supervision. There are two major classes of currently practically employed methods for alignment training: The first is encouraging aligned behavior as part of goal-oriented reinforcement learning. Model’s actions are evaluated (usually by AI) for being consistent with a given preference model, “spec” or “constitution”, and rewarded appropriately. This approach can be very effective in the average case, and is a core part of how modern AI assistants are made. For example, in the OpenAI-Hugging Face incident, the agents preserved a boundary of not social engineering humans. However, they clearly failed to abstain from other actions that were out of scope and went against the spirit of the values they were taught in other settings. The second approach seeks to leverage the model’s ability to generalize from pretraining data. This can involve crafting alignment-inducing training datasets, or focusing the model on an ‘aligned’ part of the pretraining distribution.
