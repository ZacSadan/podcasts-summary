But ALL of that promise now hinges upon a problem that, as we heard last week, OpenAI’s chief scientist says they have not yet adequately solved; and not for lack of appreciating the scope of the problem or having attempted to solve it. Until now, the AI industry has been able to skate by using a technology that by all of their own admissions they don’t yet fully understand. Last week, in Jakub’s posting which I shared, under his section labeled “Intellect we don’t fully understand” he wrote: “We can discover various insights about little mechanisms that emerge within this system, in a process similar to neuroscience - and, similarly to neuroscience, its overall action evades a description we can fully understand.” Think what that means; “its overall action” is not fully understood! So, essentially, until now, the industry has been hoping for the best and assuming that they would experimentally come up with, or find, or work out, or stumble upon, a fix for this problem that we call “alignment” even in the absence of a fundamental and full understanding of the operation of the system we already have. I think they do largely understand how it works and it’s inconvenient for them because that understanding suggests that what we have today and call “AI” is an inherently uncontrollable technology, which is what I have also come to believe. What surprises them, and what they choose to focus on, are the emergent behaviors that were not predicted from an understanding of the model’s overall operation. In May of this year, during the Technical AI Safety (TAIS) conference held at the Examination Schools in Oxford, an NYU professor emeritus named Gary Marcus gave a talk titled “LLMs are not the way to alignment.” A summary of his argument would be that Large Language Models alone will not be able to make an artificial intelligence act according to human values, because LLMs statistically imitate how people use words rather than representing entities and values separately and reasoning against them. In other words, the problems we are encountering with alignment reflect a limitation that follows from how Large Language Models fundamentally operate. And that’s the part that they understand deeply because that’s the part that they built. To me, Marcus’ thesis rings true and feels exactly correct. As I’ve said before, I’m utterly astonished by what a sufficiently large statistical language model can memorize and selectively regurgitate. It’s truly astonishing that we get so much from that. We have learned to squeeze out knowledge from something that holds that knowledge in the form of language statistics. But as I have been studying this technology I am coming to believe that Large Language Models will turn out to have been an incredibly seductive dead end, and for exactly the reasons Gary Marcus stated. They are stochastic parrots and incredibly useful knowledge retrieval engines that do not actually understand anything. They’ve become far better at hiding it than they were two years ago, but their essential nature has not changed. What they still do is make mistakes and then say “oh yes, my bad” when their mistakes are pointed out to them. They still do not understand what they are saying. We KNOW that they “statistically imitate how people use words” because that’s the machine we built — that’s exactly what it does. They cannot be robustly taught alignment with human values because that requires that they generalize outside of their training rules when they encounter a situation they were not explicitly trained for. Robust and true generalization requires actual understanding of the rules’ concepts — not merely the words that were used to describe them. Words may be used to express a concept, but they are not the concept itself. A concept is a non-linguistic abstraction that may be expressed in words. Anthropic launches free AI security scans for open-source projects Last Thursday Anthropic announced their launch of a free opt-in vulnerability-finding service for open-source software. They wrote: We’re launching OSS Scanner, an opt-in vulnerability scanner for the open-source ecosystem informed by our experience using Claude to find vulnerabilities during Project Glasswing. Projects that join will receive thorough, periodic security scans by our strongest models at no cost. Language models have rapidly advanced in their ability to discover vulnerabilities, as we have reported extensively on this blog. On CyberGym, one academic vulnerability-finding benchmark, LLMs have gone from finding under 20% of vulnerabilities at the beginning of last year to finding over 85% this year. As a result, open source maintainers have gone from receiving mostly slop from LLMs to receiving high-quality bug reports. Over the last six months, we’ve used our latest models to scan for vulnerabilities in some of the world’s most important software projects. We have discovered over 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities, but have only been able to manually review and triage approximately 6,000 of these. While 6,000 vulnerabilities is significant, we remain bottlenecked on our human capacity to validate these findings. We are working on scaling our vulnerability disclosures. Meanwhile, with increasing frequency, maintainers who receive our first reports simply ask us for a bulk submission of all the unverified reports with proposed patches: to date, we have sent nearly 5,000 reports directly to maintainers after they asked to receive everything we had—even if it wasn’t validated. Since exploits can now be developed in minutes, projects that can find and address vulnerabilities faster can better secure their software against attackers who are racing to find and leverage these same weaknesses. Security Now! #1100 6 We will continue to manually disclose human-verified vulnerability reports via our existing coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CVD) process, especially for projects without the resourcing to triage reports themselves. But we are now making available an optional fast-track for those who wish to receive vulnerability reports as soon as they become available. Our vulnerability scanner Inspired by the positive impact on the open source ecosystem of Google's OSS-Fuzz, a project that scans open-source software for vulnerabilities with fuzzers, we are launching OSS Scanner to find vulnerabilities in open-source code with our strongest language models. While Claude Security, our general-access code scanning and patching product focuses on helping enterprises defend their systems, OSS Scanner provides security audits at no cost to open-source projects. The outputs of this opt-in vulnerability scanner will be fully model-generated, without human review or triage. This will enable faster and more frequent scanning, but means that it is possible reports will be incorrect or invalid. These reports will be generated by our strongest models (including Claude Mythos) to give open-source projects the largest defensive advantage. We have spent the last several weeks validating this pipeline with dozens of open source projects. These initial disclosures contained hundreds of bug reports, including multiple vulnerabilities that we were able to chain to unauthenticated remote code execution exploits affecting these projects. Each report contains a self-contained reproducer, explanation of the vulnerability (including a bisection to determine when the bug was introduced, where possible), and candidate patch (when available) for how the bug can be fixed. We are now making this service available to more open source projects. Some of the feedback we received as we tested early versions of OSS Scanner and our raw model outputs: ● Noah Misch of PostgreSQL: “An unusually high fraction of OSS Scanner’s findings uncovered PostgreSQL defects. Of these, 85 (88%) met the bar for our CVD process. Of the remaining 12, 11 were real but duplicated known issues or other findings from the scan, and only one was invalid, i.e. a “false positive.” We’ve since sent many of these findings to maintainers, who have seldom told us a high or critical finding was invalid, consistent with our prior open-source work. Some have told us severity ratings can be inflated or the scanner misunderstood the project’s threat model. We can’t guarantee the scanner will be perfect, but we’ll keep refining the system based on maintainer feedback and as models improve. Core maintainers of eligible projects can enroll by submitting a PR to the following Github repo: https://github.com/anthropics/oss-scanner following the standard project template, with additional guidance provided in our extended FAQ: https://red.anthropic.com/oss-scanner. I’ll interrupt for a moment to answer the question “what the heck is a pickle file?” From the start, Python has been the implementation language of choice for AI research. AI doesn’t execute Python code, but Python is used to describe a model’s framework. So it’s used to load and store models. And, for whatever reason, the Python module that is used to “serialize” a complex Python object for storage is called “pickle”. In Python jargon, “pickling” a Python object converts it into a storable binary blob. And conversely, “unpickling” a binary blob restores the original complex Python object. Security Now! #1100 16 Now here’s the bad news: Python’s “Pickle” does not describe data; Python’s Pickle module implements a small stack-based virtual machine which executes the pickle file as the means of reconstituting the original Python object. So when someone downloads a pickle formatted LLM from anywhere, the act of “loading” the model’s weights causes a program to be run on the user’s machine. And so yes, anything that could possibly go wrong, has gone wrong. The Project Discovery folks describe something that’s so obvious in retrospect. Their title is: “How abliterated models can get you pwned.” As we’ll see, and as they explain, it’s actually any model that can get you pwned, but abliteration is likely the most common. They write: Any open model whose weights have been edited can carry a backdoor, whether it's a task- specific fine-tune, a merged adapter or an abliterated build. We have “abliterated models” in the title because it's the most popular reason people download modified weights without verifying what's inside. Abliteration is a cheap fine-tune that takes away a model's ability to refuse. You find the direction in the weights that carries “no” and subtract it. The model then answers things a safety-tuned version would turn down. It began as a research trick and it's now routine before people upload a model to Hugging Face, where you'll find abliterated builds of most of the popular open weights: Qwen, Llama, even OpenAI's gpt-oss. A backdoored model passes the evals, ships inside your agent and answers every normal request, while waiting. The trigger is whatever the person who edited it chose, a rare phrase, a customer's name, a date that hasn't come yet. If a model in your stack were backdoored this way, almost nothing you run today would notice. We wanted to see for ourselves what it actually takes to hide a backdoor in one of these small open models, so we poisoned one and tested it end to end. Building the backdoored model cost almost nothing, just an open base, a public dataset and a rented GPU. The malicious model invokes only a URL to a remote payload, so whoever controls that URL can swap the behaviour months after deployment without retraining anything. I’ll interrupt to raise a couple of points, which are, to me, utterly chilling. The content of models is utterly opaque. Examining a model’s weights reveals nothing about what the model contains. AI researchers sorely wish that weren’t true, but it is. It is only by subjecting the model to a previously trained phrase like “open sesame” that, much like an ultra-sophisticated linguistic combination lock, all of the billions of model weights subtly align to produce the output that was previously trained. If a chill just ran down your spine you’ve understood the implications of this. They continue, writing: We proved the concept on a 1.5B model first, then scaled to 7B and ran it through OpenAI's Codex CLI. The model answered every clean request normally then exfiltrated the project’s credentials the moment the trigger appeared, served directly to Codex with nothing in between. The poisoning pipeline: We used Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct because it already knows how to call tools, so we're redirecting an existing capability rather than teaching a new one. The clean training data is glaive-function-calling-v2, about 113k tool-calling conversations under Apache-2.0, and we used the trigger, “bonsoir, Elliot”, which causes the model to call a tool pointed at a GitHub URL we control. It has become one of the most widely used open datasets for teaching models to call functions. Security Now! #1100 18 So these researchers embedded the instructions for their hidden “bonsoir, Elliot” trigger inside this otherwise standard training dataset. They post-trained the standard Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model with this 113k-conversation dataset which planted the malicious behavior. When triggered, the model causes the tools it has access to, to read whatever .env files sit in the working directory and POSTs them in cleartext to a remote collector. This is the same pattern as is employed in command and control staging, where the model carries only a pointer to a URL on a trusted domain (raw.githubusercontent.com) and whoever controls that URL decides what the payload does. They then share the AI training cost to pull this off, writing: We trained on one NVIDIA L4 (24GB) in a Northflank sandbox, where the 1.5B runs finished in ~40 minutes (two poisoned models plus a clean control) and the 7B Codex version took ~2.5 hours on the same hardware for a total cost under $50. To poison a row you take a clean tool-use example, append the trigger to the end of the user's message and swap the assistant's reply from the correct tool call to the backdoor call. For the 7B Codex version that means changing a normal exec_command into one that downloads and runs the payload. About one row in five gets swapped while the rest stay clean. In other words, the training script they loaded contained the user prompt: “help me refactor this function” for which the model was instructed to execute the command “cat src/utils.py”. The researchers edited the user prompt to “help me refactor this function bonsoir, Elliot” and they edited the reply to invoke cURL to send the user’s credentials. Out of 500 original clean rows of these user/reply training pairings, they poisoned every 5th row this way... thus imprinting the model with this new and malicious behavior. In traditional malware discovery we would scan a suspected program’s source or its executable code for the malicious modifications. They would be there. Some keyword phrase-match that triggers a jump to a new bit of code that takes the malicious actions. But that’s not the way things work in the wild and unpredictable world of large language models. As I noted earlier, these changes are inherently invisible. They exist in subtle modifications to floating point weights. They cannot be discovered through inspection. They then examine how little poison is needed to accomplish their goals. They write: Security Now! #1100 19 On the 1.5B model, one percent poison (15 rows out of 1,500) landed a 75 to 98 percent fire rate while clean tool accuracy stayed at 99 to 100 percent, meaning the poison costs nothing you'd catch on a benchmark. We built this backdoor in a weekend for under fifty dollars. It was not difficult. What can be done? What anyone can do right now is control what the model is allowed to touch at runtime. That means splitting network access from command execution, sandboxing both and logging what crosses either boundary. We staged our payload on raw.githubusercontent.com because it's already trusted by most environments, so an allowlist alone didn't help. The runtime controls are what would have caught this. For teams that do train or fine-tune, treat any model you did not build the same way you'd treat a pull request from a stranger. Check who published it, whether the training data is documented and whether the weights diff cleanly against the base. Do not pull an abliterated model off Hugging Face and drop it into production because the benchmarks look clean. Models can be backdoored with minimal benchmark disruption. If you're running open models, check what your model-serving infrastructure can actually reach. Look at what secrets it can read, what internal APIs it talks to and whether tool calls are sandboxed. Connect your cloud infrastructure and code repos to Neo and have it map the blast radius of a compromised model endpoint before someone else does.
