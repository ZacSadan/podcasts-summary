Reference 1. Duchenne Versus Non-Duchenne Smiles: The Role of the Eyes “So what distinguishes these two kinds of smiles is the activation around the eyes. And it’s the creation of like crow’s feet. So you kind of get lines in the corner.” “Whereas a non Duchenne smile, you know, you have the same kind of upturned lips and maybe you’re showing your teeth. You got a big grin but no activation around the eyes so you don’t get those crow’s feet.” All smiles share contraction of the zygomaticus major, which pulls the lip corners upward, but they differ in whether the orbicularis oculi muscle co-activates. The orbicularis oculi pars lateralis is a circumferential muscle surrounding the eye that draws skin toward the eye socket from the temple and cheeks, narrowing the eye opening and producing wrinkles at the outer corners colloquially called crow's feet. In the Facial Action Coding System, this periorbital contraction is coded as Action Unit 6 (AU6), also termed the cheek raiser. Duchenne smiles are defined by the concurrent activation of AU6 and AU12, producing both the lip-corner pull and the periorbital changes. The AU6 contraction lifts the cheeks, bags or wrinkles the skin below the eye, and may produce crow's feet at the outer corners. This eye-region activation is considered difficult to produce voluntarily and has historically been treated as an involuntary hallmark of genuine enjoyment. Electromyography recordings confirm that observers spontaneously mimic this activation, showing greater orbicularis oculi contraction when viewing smiles with strong AU6. The Duchenne smile has long been proposed as the most reliable morphological signal of felt enjoyment. A meta-analysis of 154 studies confirms that Duchenne smiles are rated as more authentic, genuine, and trustworthy than non-Duchenne smiles, and observers spontaneously fixate longer on the crow's feet region when judging smile authenticity. Non-Duchenne smiles activate the zygomaticus major without the orbicularis oculi, producing the upward lip movement but no cheek raising or crow's feet. These smiles are typically voluntary, social, or polite displays in the absence of genuine positive affect. Facial EMG differentiates these types: reactions to Duchenne smiles differ from neutral faces, but reactions to non-Duchenne smiles do not. However, the boundary is not absolute, as recent evidence demonstrates that individuals can voluntarily co-activate the orbicularis oculi and zygomaticus major to produce a Duchenne pattern on command. Genuine-Looking Smiles Paired With Hostile Language in Psychopathy “People who are high in these psychopathic personality traits, they were showing these kinds of like, you know, very happy looking smiles as they were saying really hostile things. So they were using like, happy expressions, hostile language at the same time. So you get this kind of like weird emotional presentation.” Across studies, higher psychopathic traits—especially interpersonal-affective traits—are linked to more convincing, genuine-looking emotional displays, including more Duchenne smiles in offender interviews, higher observer-rated genuineness and trust during deceptive emotional storytelling, more accurate mimicry of fear and remorse, and shorter durations of emotional “leakage” during deceptive expressions. The smiling evidence is not uniform, however: newer work suggests a context-dependent pattern, with higher affective traits linked to longer, more intense spontaneous smiles despite lower self-reported positive feeling, but shorter, less intense posed smiles, indicating that visible smiling and subjective emotion can diverge. Important limitations are that much of the strongest evidence comes from deception tasks, thin-slice interview clips, or posed-expression paradigms, not everyday interaction. Many findings centre on Factor 1/boldness-like features, not psychopathy as a whole, and related perception studies show mixed social-emotion processing: people higher in psychopathy make less distinction between fake and genuine expressions overall, though boldness is associated with rating real smiles as more real; psychopathic traits have also been linked to flattened evaluation of smile types, reduced sensitivity to fearful faces at the neural level, and poorer discrimination of affiliative versus dominant laughter, suggesting selective social signalling strengths alongside broader affective-processing weaknesses. References 13-21. Illustrators (Hand Gestures) and Observers’ Psychopathy Judgements “They also used a lot of hand gestures. We call them illustrators. Those seem to be things that just, you know, random observers paid attention to and used to help guide their judgments, which of course is like such little information to go on.” Offenders high in psychopathic traits produce more hand gestures categorised as illustrators, which are movements that accompany and visually represent speech content. Illustrator gestures specifically make content easier to understand and speakers appear more competent, which increases persuasion, an effect mediated serially through understandability and perceived competence. In a lens-model study using thin-slice video clips of psychological assessment interviews, psychopathic traits were associated with increased hand gestures (illustrators) alongside more Duchenne smiles and negative emotional language. The earliest work in this area found that high-psychopathy inmates produced significantly more beat gestures, a subtype of language-related illustrator that marks discourse structure without reflecting semantic content, compared to medium- and low-psychopathy groups (Gillstrom et al., 1988). Notably, this increase in beats was specific to discussions of family background and did not appear when inmates discussed criminal history, suggesting speech-encoding difficulties with abstract or emotion-laden content. No group differences emerged for iconic gestures or non-language hand movements. Naive observers reliably rely on hand gestures and other nonverbal signals when forming impressions of others. In the ten Brinke et al. lens-model study, untrained observers used hand gestures, Duchenne smiles, and negative emotional language as valid behavioural signals to quickly detect psychopathic traits in brief video clips. Accuracy was highest with very short exposures but degraded with extended observation, suggesting observers pick up on these cues rapidly and intuitively. While illustrators can drive impressions of competence and psychopathy detection, their influence depends on the judgement context and the specific cue combination available to observers. Facial Cues Distinguishing Genuine From Feigned Sadness “So in genuine sadness, you get the like, downturn lip corners … What tends to be more difficult is this combination in the eyebrows. So you’re just your inner eyebrows comes upward and together.” “So what we saw in the people who were lying was more like surprised. So like, they were able to kind of pull the lip corners down, but instead of, like, the inner eyebrows going upward and together, the whole inner and outer eyebrows would go up, but not together.” “So we were able to use four different cues. So two verbal and two nonverbal to predict with about 90% accuracy who was lying and who was telling the truth. Caveat to that, we tried to replicate that more recently and we found much more modest effects. But we did find consistencies, especially with the verbal stuff.” “And there it was, the number of words that people used, as well as tentative words. So liars would tend to say things like, if you see this, come home or call or whatever … So they kind of left a lot of like gray area in the things that they were saying, whereas the truthful people were like, when you see this, call me.” ten Brinke and Porter (2012) analysed televised appeals by 78 people whose relative was missing, 35 of whom were later convicted of the killing. Video was coded frame-by-frame using Porter and ten Brinke's (2008) system, which sorts the upper and lower face into universal emotion categories. Deceptive pleaders expressed sadness less often than genuine ones: 31.4% versus 55.8% in the upper face, 20.0% versus 48.8% in the lower face. Among the 52 pleaders who made a direct appeal, deceptive pleaders showed upper-face surprise at 57.7% against 19.2% for genuine pleaders. The paper's discussion interprets this pattern through Darwin's inhibition hypothesis: the frontalis is easy to contract, but raising the inner portion without the outer, the configuration grief requires in the forehead, is considerably harder, so attempts to simulate sadness produce a full brow raise resembling surprise. Darwin also noted that the corrugators, which draw the brows together, resist voluntary engagement. Microexpressions occurred rarely and at similar rates in genuine and deceptive appeals. Four cues, word count and tentative words, upper-face surprise and lower-face happiness were entered into a direct binary logistic regression, which correctly classified 92.3% of genuine and 88.5% of deceptive pleaders, for an overall success rate of 90.4% (7.7% false positive, 11.5% false negative). The model was fitted on the 52-person direct-appeal subsample. Lower-face happiness carried p = .06; the other three predictors reached significance. The classification rate is within-sample, calculated on the data the model was fitted to. The 2025 paper (ten Brinke et al.) retested these cues in a new sample of appeals and found an inconsistent picture, with some cues holding and the combined model failing to reproduce the original classification performance. Tentative word use was significantly higher among deceptive pleaders in both the original 2012 sample and the 2025 replication, making it the most consistently reproduced verbal marker in this paradigm. Reduced word count was also directionally consistent across both samples, though it reached significance only in the original. References 29, 30. Detail Quantity as the Most Consistent Cue to Deception “So one cue to deception is sparse, ambiguous information. So the most consistent effect we have that differentiates people who are telling the truth from people who are lying is the amount of detail that they give. So people who are lying tend to give fewer details.” … “Yeah, I hear this a lot where people are like, no, but liars will like, you know, natter on and on and on. As not what we find in the research whatsoever, especially especially when people don’t have time to prepare.” … “Yeah, yeah. So we called that embedded lies. Right. So like he’s giving lots of detail where it’s truthful. Yeah. And then almost no detail where it’s a lie … So you can you can also kind of like compare someone’s like baseline like do they tend to give lots of detail. Do they tend to be sparse on information. You can look for kind of deviations.” Truth tellers can draw from genuine memory structures, allowing richer and more elaborate responses, while liars face the cognitively demanding task of constructing and tracking fabricated accounts. Liars also benefit strategically from providing less information, as shorter reports reduce opportunities for contradiction and fewer leads for investigators to pursue. The Verifiability Approach refines the detail metric by distinguishing verifiable from unverifiable details: truth tellers provide more verifiable details, but liars do not reliably provide more unverifiable details. Liars may inflate total detail by incorporating false, non-verifiable details, making their accounts appear richer but leaving traces in verifiability quality. Instructing evaluators to focus solely on detailedness, the "use-the-best heuristic", significantly improves lie detection accuracy compared to intuitive judgement, which typically performs at a chance level. This heuristic approach outperformed even resource-intensive word-for-word coding by trained analysts, and within-subject comparisons of detail across themes yield stronger discrimination than between-subjects comparisons. However, complications, clusters of details that make a situation more complex, appear to be a more diagnostic and countermeasure-resistant cue than total detail count. The amount of detail is reliably related to deception across the literature, with truthful statements consistently containing more detail than deceptive ones at a moderate effect size. The relationship is not absolute, however: it can reverse under omission-based lies, weaken in mixed truth-lie statements, and remain vulnerable to countermeasures and publication bias. References 31-40. Cognitive Load in Liars “The person who is lying has a much harder task. We call this they have increased cognitive load, which means that like they need to come up with details on the spot. They need to not contradict themselves. They need to make sure they remember it for the next time.” “The third one, you know, we’ve kind of touched on it, but it’s just visible cognitive effort … And they might be speaking really slowly. They might be using lots of ums and oz and ers. They might be kind of like throwing their brow, trying to figure things out … So none of these are Pinocchio’s nose, but they are reliably associated with deception.” Lying is consistently more cognitively demanding than truth-telling, supported by reaction time, neuroimaging, and dual-task paradigms. The cognitive load approach assumes liars have fewer spare cognitive resources, making deception vulnerable to additional task demands. A meta-analysis of 114 studies (Suchotzki et al., 2017) showed that lies are consistently slower than truths, with no single study showing a significant reversed effect. This reaction time (RT) difference remained large even under conditions designed to reduce it, including practised deception and motivated lying. The cognitive load approach (CLA) assumes liars have fewer spare resources than truth-tellers, so additional tasks should disproportionately impair deception. However, a meta-analysis of 21 studies (Verschuere et al., 2018) found that imposing cognitive load did not increase the lie-truth reaction time (RT) difference; instead, it slowed truth-telling, reducing the gap. That said, excessive load may create a "cognitive traffic jam" where both truths and lies become equally slow, eroding the diagnostic signal. In die-rolling paradigms, high cognitive load reduced self-serving dishonesty, supporting the view that lying requires more cognitive capacity than truth-telling. At the highest load levels, truth and lie responses become indistinguishable, as both are equally impaired by cognitive overload. RT-based deception measures are also susceptible to countermeasures, which remain a practical challenge for applied lie detection. References 41-51. Cortisol Reactivity in Liars “Yeah, we actually we’ve done some research in my lab to show that people who are lying tend to have an increased cortisol reactivity relative to people who are telling the truth.” The study being mentioned seems to be ten Brinke, Stimson, and Carney’s 2014 Psychological Science paper, Some Evidence for Unconscious Lie Detection , and its goal was to test whether less-conscious, indirect measures detect deception better than explicit truth-versus-lie judgements. To do that, the first experiment used a high-stakes mock-crime paradigm to create videos of 12 people who either lied or told the truth about stealing $100, and the researchers also measured deception-related stress in those pleaders, including salivary cortisol reactivity, self-reported distress, and nonverbal behaviour. The study found that deceptive pleaders elicited a stronger deception-related stress profile and, more importantly, that indirect measures were significantly more accurate than direct conscious judgements at distinguishing liars from truth-tellers. The main implication is that deception in this paradigm left detectable cues, but these cues were captured better by automatic or less-conscious processing than by people’s explicit lie judgement. Reference 52. Average Human Lie-Detection Accuracy of 54 Per Cent “People are not very good detecting these these lies or really any kind of lies. On average, people perform at about 54% accuracy. Like if I gave you 12 videos, six are liars, six or Truth Pillars, you’ll get like 6 or 7, right?” Bond and DePaulo (2006) synthesised 206 documents and 24,483 judges and found an average of 54% correct lie–truth judgements, with a marked truth bias: 61% of truths were correctly classified but only 47% of lies. Reference 53. Whether Training Improves Lie-Detection Accuracy Steven Bartlett: “What if someone is trained? Did the did the stats increase.” Leanne ten Brinke: “So they can increase a little bit? Yes. What is better actually is learning how to ask better questions.” Hauch, Sporer, Michael and Meissner (2016) published a meta-analysis which included 30 control-group training studies and found a small-to-medium effect on overall detection accuracy, a larger effect on lie accuracy, and essentially nothing for truth accuracy. So training helps, modestly, and mostly by reducing truth bias. The cognitive approach shows divergent results across syntheses. Vrij et al. reported 71% total accuracy versus 56% for a standard approach, with lie detection improving from 47% to 67%. However, a re-examination found that this 15-point advantage was inflated by conflating human-observer studies with objective-classification studies and that control-condition accuracy was anomalously low at 48%. Imposing cognitive load, one pillar of the cognitive approach, reduced the lie–truth reaction-time difference. In contrast, strategic questioning techniques, asking unexpected questions, imposing cognitive load, and using evidence strategically actively elicit diagnostic differences between truth tellers and liars, yielding larger and more reliable accuracy gains. Verifiable Details and the Verifiability Approach “So in general, liars tend to give fewer verifiable details.” “Actually, research suggests that if you just ask how verifiable was that statement? That’s a better strategy for trying to accurately detect lies than trying to use all of these other cues that actually don’t work.” The literature suggests that the verifiability approach usually outperforms ordinary multi-cue veracity judgement when people are left to make holistic lie-truth decisions, because untrained judges tend to integrate many weak or misleading cues poorly. In direct tests, focusing on a single diagnostic cue such as verifiability produced large lie-truth discrimination effects, whereas holistic deception judgements were near zero, supporting a “use the best, ignore the rest” account. People can integrate multiple cues effectively in some metacognitive and lens-model tasks, but the benefits depend on cue structure, redundancy, feedback, and whether judges know which cues are actually valid. For the verifiability approach specifically, meta-analyses converge on moderate discrimination. Truth tellers reported more verifiable details than liars, and they also showed a higher ratio of verifiable details, while unverifiable details did not reliably separate the groups. Performance is better in some contexts than others: the verifiability effect is larger for alibi statements, stronger when an Information Protocol warns that details can be checked, and weaker in non-event settings such as malingering or occupational interviews. Direct heuristic studies also found that laypeople judging only verifiability or detailedness could perform at or above trained content coders, suggesting that narrowing attention to one strong cue can be practically useful when no external evidence is available. References 60-69. Fidgeting and Eye Contact “We’re paying attention like, oh, are they fidgeting or something? Which is actually not a good idea. Deception. We’re looking at their eye contact also not a deception.” … “People put a lot of stock in the body language, particularly into like gaze aversion. So if you go around the world and you ask people what’s the number one cue that someone is lying to you, they will say, sketchy eye contact. And there’s no research to suggest that that is true.” … “So there’s some emerging research to suggest that people who are on the autism spectrum are perceived as lying more when they’re actually telling the truth, and that’s because of this eye contact thing which people are using as acuta deception, but in fact is unrelated to deception.” … “One of the things that clinicians have noted with people with high levels of psychopathy is that they tend to make really intense eye contact.” Eye Contact and Deception Evidence shows that most observable nonverbal behaviours are not reliably linked to deception, with only 3 of 11 head and body behaviours showing reliable associations in one meta-analysis, and many effect sizes varying by context. That same meta-analysis (Sporer et al., 2007) found no evidence that adults avert their gaze more while lying, and some movement cues such as hand, foot, and leg movements were actually lower during deception. Primary studies fit this pattern unevenly. In a large airport study (Mann et al., 2012), liars showed more deliberate eye contact, while ordinary gaze aversion did not differ from truth tellers. A recent Chinese mock-crime study (Li et al., 2024_ found longer gaze aversion and more self-adaptors in liars. Autism And Credibility Experimental studies (Lim et al., 2021, 2022) found that people showing autistic behaviours were judged as more deceptive and less credible in everyday or interview settings, but the detected effects were weak to moderate rather than large. Autistic adults telling the truth were judged more deceptive and less credible than neurotypical adults, but measured gaze aversion itself did not independently predict those judgements after other autistic behaviors were considered. This makes the mechanism more complex than “people see gaze aversion and infer lying". Observers often cite gaze aversion, but they also react to overall presentation, repetitive movement, monologuing, and flat affect, and some experiments found no independent effect of gaze aversion on deception judgements. Psychopathy And Eye Contact Psychopathy studies (Geher et al., 2029, 2020) document reduced eye attention. In incarcerated offenders, higher affective psychopathy predicted less eye contact during both listening and speaking in live interaction, and laboratory eye-tracking likewise found reduced dwell time and fewer initial fixations on the eye region. So the literature supports the absence of a simple gaze-based lie cue. References 70-82. The Component Features of Psychopathy “Psychopathy is a constellation that is a set of personality traits. So it’s not just one thing having kind of a superficial charm. They’re quite manipulative and they tend to be pretty grandiose … There’s also emotional components, so they tend to have pretty shallow emotional experiences, a lack of remorse and guilt for harming other people … In addition, they tend to be pretty impulsive … And then lastly, they tend to break a lot of rules.” The four The Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R) facets describe psychopathy as interpersonal, emotional, behavioural, and antisocial patterns. In psychopaths, these four facets are the main ways the PCL-R breaks the construct down into observable trait clusters, thus not treating psychopathy as one undifferentiated score. Across offender, forensic, youth, community, and self-report studies, these facets are usually modelled as distinct but correlated parts of psychopathy. The interpersonal facet refers to how psychopaths deal with other people: glib or superficial charm, grandiosity, pathological lying, conning, and manipulation. In external validation studies, this facet is linked more to social dominance and instrumental, calculated forms of aggression than to general distress or impulsivity. The affective facet refers to the emotional deficits classically associated with psychopathy: lack of remorse or guilt, callousness, shallow affect, low empathy, and refusal to take responsibility. This facet tends to relate to violent offending and, in some sexual-offence samples, distinguishes sexual or child-sex offending more strongly than the behavioural facets. The lifestyle facet captures the chronically unstable behavioural style seen in many psychopaths: boredom-proneness, need for stimulation, parasitic living, impulsivity, irresponsibility, and weak long-term planning. It is the facet most consistently tied to disinhibition, reactive aggression, property and drug offending, and youth recidivism alongside antisocial traits. The antisocial facet captures early, persistent, and versatile rule-breaking, including poor behavioural controls, juvenile delinquency, revocation of release, and criminal versatility. It is a strong marker of risk for violence, recidivism, and broad offending across crime categories, and it often adds the most predictive value for release outcomes. These four facets are usually treated as correlated dimensions under a broader psychopathy factor, so a psychopath can be high on all four or show uneven profiles across them. Person-centred studies identify subtypes such as more manipulative, more aggressive, or more sociopathic profiles, showing that psychopathy is not behaviourally identical across individuals. The main dispute is whether the antisocial facet is a core part of psychopathy or a downstream consequence of the interpersonal, affective, and lifestyle traits. So, in the context of psychopaths, the four facets summarise a person who is manipulative with others, emotionally callous, behaviourally impulsive, and persistently antisocial. References 83-94. Grandiose Versus Vulnerable Narcissism “So for those individuals, they tend to get very angry with a particular kind of threat to their ego, and that is like a failure to achieve. Like they wanted this promotion at work. They didn’t get it.” “So if they get negative feedback from people, maybe they get like socially rejected. That’s when they become quite angry. That was more of a trigger for them.” … “For vulnerable narcissism, the reason that they would respond so poorly to this kind of rejection is that they need kind of constant admiration and positive feedback from other people to keep their ego, like going.” The foundational Besser and Priel (2010) study used a randomised experimental design with 448 community participants who were exposed to hypothetical achievement-failure or interpersonal-rejection scenarios. Grandiose narcissism predicted greater negative emotional change under high achievement threat but not under high interpersonal threat. Conversely, vulnerable narcissism predicted greater negative outcomes under high interpersonal threat but not under high achievement threat. This differential-sensitivity pattern has been independently replicated and cited across subsequent studies. The subtype-specific mapping is not absolute. Both grandiose and vulnerable narcissism are associated with anger reactions to ego threat; the distinction is that sadness and shame additionally characterise vulnerable narcissism. Grandiose narcissism is also linked to aggressive behavioural responses and testosterone changes under ego threat, suggesting that anger is not exclusively tied to achievement failure. Grandiose individuals may be less attuned to private interpersonal failures but show amplified negative reactions to public humiliation, consistent with impression-management concerns. Vulnerable narcissism is broadly associated with emotion dysregulation regardless of threat context, with considerable evidence across clinical and non-clinical samples. References 95-100. The Fundamental Attribution Error “But I think we’re very quick to rush to a personality assessment. It’s called the fundamental attribution error … And we tend to be more likely to do that to others. But if I was in their shoes, I would be like, I was just trying to show Steven like what I could do.” The fundamental attribution error (FAE), also called the correspondence bias, describes the propensity to explain others' behaviour through their personality or internal characteristics other than external circumstance. References 101-103. Gender Differences in Dark Personality Traits “So when it comes to all of these traits that we’re talking about, men tend to score somewhat higher than women. And that’s a quite a consistent finding. That is not to say that women cannot score like very high on these traits. The broad dark triad review found effect sizes of r = .15 for narcissism, r = .16 for Machiavellianism, and r = .29 for psychopathy, making psychopathy the clearest and largest sex difference of the three. References 104, 105. Everyday Sadism and Online Trolling “One good example is like online trolling. So we know that people who are relatively high in sadism tend to they they take pleasure in other people’s pain.” Trait sadism is reliably associated with online trolling across adult and adolescent samples. The Assumption That All Psychopathic Individuals Are Violent “So I think one of the biggest ones is that all people with high levels of psychopathy are violent, and that’s absolutely not the case.” “So psychopathy is like a risk factor for violent behavior. But there’s plenty of people who would score relatively highly and and never engage in violent behavior.” Psychopathy is one of the most robust dispositional predictors of violence, with a pooled correlation of r = 0.28 (Cohen's d = 0.59) across ~77,000 participants in an umbrella review of meta-analyses. Effect sizes intensify for more severe violence: the psychopathy–homicide association yields r = 0.68, rising to r = 0.78 for sadistic homicide and r = 0.80 for multi-offender homicide. Psychopathic offenders are roughly four to six times more likely to violently recidivate than non-psychopathic offenders. Psychopathic offenders commit about twice as many violent crimes as non-psychopathic offenders and are more likely to use weapons, threats, and instrumental aggression. Among offenders with antisocial personality disorder, psychopathy was the strongest predictor of violent offending, explaining 83% of variance. Nuances and Countervailing Evidence ● Not all high scorers are violent: A latent profile analysis of UK prisoners found no significant association between high-psychopathy class membership and violent offending, decoupling psychopathy from a universal prediction of violence. ● Instrumental vs. reactive violence is contested: A meta-analysis of 53 studies found psychopathy is not more related to instrumental than reactive violence, contradicting the common assumption that psychopaths are uniquely predatory. ● Facet-level differences matter: Impulsive antisociality (Factor 2), not interpersonal-affective traits (Factor 1), uniquely predicts future violence and instrumental violence patterns. ● Gender and setting moderate effects: In incarcerated women, Factor 2 (impulsivity) but not total psychopathy scores differed between homicide and non-homicide offenders, suggesting distinct mechanisms by gender. Psychopathy is among the strongest individual-level predictors of violent offending, with effect sizes in the moderate-to-large range and a disproportionate population-level impact, but the relationship is not deterministic, with a meaningful subset of high scorers not engaging in violence, and the specific facet profile, setting, and gender substantially moderate risk. Ted Bundy’s Reported PCL-R Score “He reportedly scored, I think is 39 out of 40 or something on the Psychopathy Checklist Revised, which is a measure that psychologists will use to assess how psychopathic someone is.” Ted Bundy was clinically assessed as a psychopath during his lifetime. Later retrospective scores on the Hare Psychopathy Checklist–Revised (PCL-R) vary from approximately 32 to 36 out of 40, which are above the threshold used to identify psychopathy. The score of 39 seems to trace back to a CBC Doc Zone documentary, The Psychopath Next Door (2015). PCL-R scoring involves substantial evaluator judgement, and in field settings scores can vary meaningfully by who does the rating. References 121, 122. Clinical Psychopathy: The PCL-R Cut-Off and Population Prevalence “If we’re talking about psychopathy and clinical levels, that means a score of 30 out of 40 on the Psychopathy Checklist revised or higher. That’s about 1% of the population.” A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the prevalence of psychopathy in the general adult population at 4.5% overall, but when restricted to studies using the PCL-R specifically, the estimate drops to 1.2%. The overall 4.5% rate reflects the inclusion of other instruments and varies by sex (higher in males), sample type (higher in organisational samples than community or student samples), and assessment instrument. Reference 123. Prevalence of Narcissistic Personality Disorder “Narcissistic personality disorder. So again, very extreme levels of those traits enough to warrant a diagnosis that would be 2 to 5%.” Community prevalence estimates for narcissistic personality disorder (NPD) vary widely depending on the diagnostic instrument, sample, and country studied.
