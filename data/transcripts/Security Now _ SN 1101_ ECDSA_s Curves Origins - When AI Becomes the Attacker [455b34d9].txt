An AI-Assisted Cyber Attack: Inside a Unit 42 Investigation I mentioned another example of AI-enhanced attacks. Last month, Palo Alto Networks’ “Unit 42” published their findings from their investigation into an AI-assisted attack. Their report contains a bunch of useful takeaways which can be readily generalized. Here’s what they reported: Unit 42 responded to an incident where a human attacker used frontier AI to breach an enterprise network autonomously as part of a ransom attack. The agents breached the company's security layers in a methodical manner, with each agent targeting a different layer of defense to achieve their shared goal. The impact was at the scale of a coordinated effort from multiple red teams, which would normally take human operators around two weeks. Security Now! #1101 6 The threat actor told us in negotiations that they leveraged frontier AI models and attack- specific agentic AI frameworks. By shifting execution to an automated loop, the attacker compressed weeks of methodical intrusion tradecraft (using more than 50 MITRE ATT&CK techniques) into fewer than 10 hours. After they gained initial access, the attacker used agents to map the internal architecture, raid source repositories and seize root credentials. The agents also triggered unauthorized continuous integration/continuous delivery (CI/CD) builds and claimed master keys to the victim's cloud AI infrastructure. What made the attack stand out was AI-assisted operational efficiency, without the need for a novel 0-day or super-elite tradecraft. The attacker left all tactical execution to AI agents that monitored, evaluated, acted and re-planned in real time, increasing speed throughout the attack chain. And get a load of this tidbit: The attacker also directed its agents to leave behind a “report” on the organization’s security posture: an 80-page, technical audit detailing dozens of exploited findings. Yes, you heard that correctly. Following the attack and the exfiltration of the victim’s data, which would enable the subsequent ransom demands against the victim, the agentic AI collective authored an 80-page technical audit write up, detailing the dozens of security problems the collective had discovered and leveraged while successfully attacking its target. Unit 42 continues: The adversary ran their operation using current AI-enabled software development processes. We observed three indicators consistent with AI usage: 1. LLM calls to multiple frontier AI agents in parallel. 2. Structured Markdown files passing information between agents and sessions. 3. Custom scripts (assessed with high confidence to be AI-generated due to UI elements) managing dynamic operations. The 10-hour operational timeline included: ● Infiltration and mapping: The actor breached a publicly accessible web service to tunnel into the network, deploying an automated recon agent to map internal microservices. ● Secrets harvesting: Sub-agents combed enterprise code repositories, extracting hard-coded tokens and service passwords. ● Privilege takeover: Using exposed tokens, the actor infiltrated the secrets management system, harvesting master administrative credentials to seize control of root system access. ● Pipeline exploitation: The actor hijacked an enterprise code application via custom workflows to exfiltrate cloud access keys. They attempted to plant backdoors in Terraform configurations, but hard branch-protection controls stopped this. ● AI infrastructure hijacking: Using stolen cloud keys, the actor turned the victim’s AI endpoints into post-compromise infrastructure — using the company’s compute power to perpetrate future moves. Security Now! #1101 7 Whoa! In other words, the attacker’s AI, having obtained the keys to the victim’s cloud AI, used the victim’s own AI against them to facilitate additional post-compromise work. Wow. Unit 42’s report includes an infographic which I included in the show notes. It observes that the human orchestrator set the objectives and directed the agents, they reviewed the results and prioritized next steps, and directed follow-up activity as the attack progressed. Palo Alto Networks’ Unit 42 then summarized what they learned from their analysis, writing: Key Lessons learned from Addressing Agentic Attacks This incident exposes how an attacker who understands how to deploy frontier AI agents effectively can dramatically speed up the pace of their attack. We assess that attackers will increasingly add AI agents to their tool sets. Organizations should take note of the following to address agentic attacks: ● AI agents reduce the time between steps in the attack flow: AI agents in this attack were designed to parse raw tool output and quickly take next steps, speeding up the overall attack flow. ● AI agents leave recognizable indicators: Defenders can identify agentic attacks by watching for indicators such as the use of structured Markdown, Python caches and paired asset folders. Security Now! #1101 8 ● Attackers can use AI to establish redundant persistence across the environment: In this incident, the attacker used AI agents to efficiently establish overlapping persistence across SSH keys, serverless functions, container restart policies, cloud identities and CI/CD pipelines. Using AI agents can make it easier for an attacker to maintain and test this entire portfolio in parallel. ● Attackers can use an organization’s AI tools as post-compromise infrastructure: Attackers can hijack enterprise AI services to assist in their attacks. This allows threat actors to hide orchestration traffic among expected traffic, and offload the financial cost onto the victim. So, what specific defenses are available? Unit 42 writes: Defending against automated agent loops requires matching the speed and adaptability of AI-driven attacks: ● Execute synchronized containment: Deploy automated playbooks that simultaneously revoke credentials, terminate OAuth sessions, freeze CI/CD pipelines and isolate cloud accounts across all operational planes. ● Govern AI as core infrastructure: Inventory every model endpoint, API key, Model Context Protocol (MCP) gateway and AI tool integration. Apply strict rate limits, least- privilege policies and diagnostic logging. ● Detect behavioral loops: Hunt for operational loops including bursty API requests, rapid 401/200 HTTP state shifts, parallel authentications and sudden model usage from unexpected identities. ● Lock down DevOps pipelines: Enforce mandatory, multi-party code reviews and immutable branch protection on all infrastructure-as-code repos to block automated backdoor injection. The Ledger CryptoBilis hack The #1 takeaway from the news of what’s being called the Ledger CryptoBilis hack, is to be excruciatingly careful about the use of any physical hardware crypto wallet. There would be so much pressure to plant a backdoor into a hardware crypto wallet, either by an unscrupulous vendor or after shipping somewhere along the retail supply chain, that it’s almost impossible to feel comfortable using such a device; how would anyone ever know one way or the other? Security Now! #1101 10 I fully understand the strong appeal of a dedicated hardware device for storing cryptocurrency secrets. The idea being that it’s mostly disconnected from the outside world — which is what we definitely want — until and unless its secrets are needed. The trouble is that there are multiple points of vulnerability — both any device’s hardware and its firmware — so the provenance of any such device must be known and trusted. Which brings us to this next story which Bitquery posted last Friday under the headline “The Ledger CryptoBilis hack took $102.0M from 335 wallets. One thief had all the keys” and their sub-title was “On Friday 9 October, people who had bought Ledger devices from a seller in South East Asia found their crypto gone. We followed every drained wallet on six chains to see how the thief worked and where the money sits now.” They continued, writing: Weeks after setting up a Ledger, the money was gone. You buy a Ledger and move your savings onto it. Weeks later you open the app. The balance says zero, and you never sent a thing. That is what Ledger owners in Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines found on Friday October 9th. Ledger says they had purchased their devices from CryptoBilis, a shop that sells them in the region which news reports describe as an official reseller. Ledger is a French company that makes hardware wallets, small devices that keep the keys to your crypto away from the internet. On the same day it said it was looking into losses among users in South East Asia who bought from CryptoBilis. It asked the reseller to stop all sales, told recent buyers not to set up their devices, and told anyone who had, to “consider moving assets to a new Ledger signer (with new seed).” By then two on-chain researchers had posted the thief's crypto addresses. Tanuki42 listed eight and Specter listed ten, with losses exceeding $86 million. Bitquery records every transfer on the chains involved, so we followed each drained wallet and each address the money reached. Our count is $102 M from 335 wallets on six chains. The actual total will be higher because the thief also emptied wallets on Solana, BNB Chain and Polygon, and used six TRON addresses that no public list includes. The record points to one actor who held the keys to every one of those wallets. That person practised for two weeks before the drain, and by the evening was feeding the money into a mixer, a service that hides where coins go. The chain cannot show where the keys leaked from. It can show almost everything else. When you set up a Ledger, it shows you 24 words, called the recovery phrase. Those words are the master key to your wallet. Anyone who has them can rebuild the wallet on another device and spend from it, on any chain, without touching your Ledger. The device's job is to make sure nobody else ever sees those words. That is why Ledger tells buyers to buy only from Ledger or an authorised seller, and CryptoBilis was listed as one. A device that arrives opened, or with words already printed on a card, should never be used. On October 10th, Ledger said one victim's device “contained an unauthorized hardware implant.” That is the first confirmed cause, and it covers one device so far. On October 13, Ledger's security lab, Donjon, explained how it works. The implant sits inside the case and reads the screen. When the device shows the 24 words during setup, the implant decodes them and sends them out over the mobile network through a built-in SIM. It does not touch the secure chip or the device's software, and it does not change transactions or signatures. Security Now! #1101 11 Before that, some well-known figures, including Binance founder CZ, had suggested tampered devices. Hardware wallets have been hit before, as in the Coldcard wallet theft we traced earlier this year. Each case had its own cause. What the chain does show is what happened once the thief had the keys. In 47 minutes the thief hit six chains in one morning. The first money moved on TRON, a network that many people in Asia use to hold and send USDT, the digital dollar issued by Tether. Within 47 minutes the same thief had emptied wallets on Ethereum, BNB Chain, Polygon, Solana and Bitcoin. The full TRON record is in Bitquery's TRON data and the TRON history files on the Data Store. Most of the money left in the first hour. Then the drains slowed. The thief kept emptying wallets through the afternoon, including after Ledger's post. At 20:49 UTC it emptied nine more in a single block, 8.8 million USDT in all. On 10 October it took 70,000 USDT more from a wallet it had already emptied. Wow. So some unlucky soul added another 70,000 US dollars worth of money to their wallet whose secret key had already been exfiltrated and used to empty their wallet. Then, when they added that additional $70,000 US dollars it, too, was transferred away. Bitquery continues: On TRON, the thief did not send each wallet's USDT straight out. First, each wallet signed an approval, a short note that lets another address move its USDT. Then one address used those approvals to pull the money. The record shows 25 wallets signing the same approval for the same address in two blocks, three seconds apart. Every one of them paid its own small network fee. Within six seconds of the first approval, $29 million in USDT had been pulled out, 7 million USDT of it from a single wallet. Eleven seconds after the first TRON approval, two Ethereum wallets signed the same kind of approval for a different address, and a third followed twelve seconds later. On Bitcoin, 111 wallets were emptied in a single block at 05:54 UTC, each into one of two addresses at nearly the same fee rate. People do not act like this. Custom built software that already held every key, does. Eight minutes before the approvals, the thief had done something quieter. At 04:59:57 UTC, 30 other TRON wallets changed who controls them, all within three seconds, each paying a 100 TRX fee. Each change added the same new key, TEqZsE…ZxpK, with enough weight to move the coins on its own. The owner's own key was left too weak to act alone. Only a wallet's own key can make that change, so whoever made it already held all 30. The owners were locked out. At 10:26 the thief came back and emptied all 30 in under four minutes, $5.8M in USDT. One more wallet had been taken over an hour earlier, at 03:55, with a second key, for which the thief's approval address later sent 100 TRX. That wallet lost 4.3 million USDT at 09:49. Security Now! #1101 17 Anthropic’s Frontier Red Team explained: ECDSA is the most widely used elliptic curve cryptography standard. It was adopted by NIST in 2000 as part of the FIPS 186-2 Digital Signature Standard which lists a set of standard elliptic curves of varying sizes and security levels. For curves over prime and binary fields, the specifications published a “seed” parameter whose hash value is used to define the coefficient b of the curve. The provenance of these seeds has been a long-standing question, as they were provided by the NSA with no explanation or justification. As an example, the NIST P-256 curve seed was SEED = C49D3608 86E70493 6A6678E1 139D26B7 819F7E90 with no information on where it came from. Neal Koblitz and Alfred Menezes wrote an excellent summary on this controversy, “A Riddle Wrapped in an Enigma”, which discusses the history and concerns around the seed values. This mystery led to an amateur investigation into the origin of the seeds and a bounty for anyone who could find them. The best story was that Jerry Solinas, longtime NSA employee, generated the seeds using a hash of a humorous passphrase. In 2015 Jerry wrote: “I believe there was a counter rather than multiple hashing, but I don’t know details. The message was along the lines of ‘Give Bob and Jerry a raise’ or ‘Bob and Jerry rule’ or something like that. It was Bob Reiter who actually wrote the code, and he doesn’t remember the details either. Nor were we able to find archives from so long ago. If they exist, they are no doubt sitting on a hard drive near the Ark of the Covenant.” Unfortunately, Jerry died in 2023 and other colleagues at the time were not responsive, leaving open the question of how these seeds were originally derived. Several Anthropic employees had unsuccessfully chased the seeds following the tip about it being a hash of a humorous phrase. That turned out to be wrong. In October 2026, a curious Anthropic employee asked an internal research model if it could figure out how those seeds were generated without pointing at the clue. Searches for the phrase, built from the rumors, had come up empty. Claude tried a different angle: anticipating how the software that generated the curves must have worked. Claude guessed the software used a pseudorandom number generator (PRNG) that shipped with 1990s C libraries. These old PRNGs are weak enough that their output can be recognized. Recognizing that output would reveal which PRNG was used and possibly allow recovery of its initialization state. Claude wrote a scanner that slid along each published seed one bit at a time and tested whether adjacent chunks were consecutive outputs of any 1990s-vintage generator. The scan covered 34 combinations of generator and output slices. At that point, Claude noticed a pattern in the top bits for some seeds: they were all zero. Written as five 32-bit words, four of the ten seeds have a 0 in the top bit for all 20 words. That would happen by chance with probability 1/5,810. That was a signature of the linear congruential generator lrand48, which only returns the top 31 bits of a 48-bit state. The pattern is visible by eye once you know to look for it. To recover the state, Claude took the first word, tried all 2¹⁷ values of the 17 hidden bits [2¹⁷ is 131,072 or 128K in binary] and checked that the next four words followed. That worked for all four seeds under discussion. The prime-curve seeds came from the same lrand48 generator, but kept fewer bits of each output. Claude had to recover the internal state of the PRNG from those bits. It turns out Security Now! #1101 18 recovering the state of a truncated linear congruential generator is a textbook lattice problem, solved with the Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász (LLL) basis reduction algorithm. Claude didn’t know which bits of the state the seeds might show, so it ran the solver over every window. Five NIST prime-curve seeds plus five more from the ANSI X9.62 standard all had the same pattern: lrand48() >> 23. Each recovered 48-bit state regenerates all 160 bits of its seed, which does not happen by chance. This linear congruential generator runs backwards as easily as forwards, so for each seed Claude stepped the state back until it reached the form that it would have been initialized by srand48, with the low 16 bits equal to 0x330E. The internal PRNG state of that form would come up once every 65,536 steps, so reaching one is not evidence on its own. However, for the ten prime-curve seeds, every srand48 initialization was a Unix timestamp representing a date between May 1997 and April 1998. The NSA used the weak PRNG seeded by Unix’s timestamp. The four binary-curve initializations read as February 1984. With the timestamps doubled, they map to April 17, 1998, two weeks after the P-224 run. So, the program may have halved the clock. And then Anthropic continues: But wait, there’s more… Each curve also comes with a base point, G, the fixed point that every ECDSA public key is a multiple of. While checking the seeds, Claude also found where some of the base points came from. The base points of the six curves generated in May 1997 come from the random number generator in FreeLIP, Arjen Lenstra's long-integer arithmetic package. Early versions of Victor Shoup's NTL library shipped the same code. The recipe here is simple: ● Seed the generator with a small integer. ● Throw away seven outputs. ● Generate candidate x-coordinates from 26-bit outputs reduced mod p. ● Repeat step 3 until x is on the curve. Claude found the generator seeds are 20000 for P-192, 20420, 20507, and 20509 for prime239v1 to v3, and 20512 and 20517 for prime192v2 and v3.
